FreshRoute

An iPad app for food pantries that shows when food is on its way, checks each delivery in at the door, and keeps proof that cold food stayed cold.

Role
UX designer and developer, on a team of four
Timeline
September to November 2026
Deliverable
Research, wireframes, style sheet, working iPad web app and a heuristic evaluation
Status
Working prototype at freshroute.pages.dev

FreshRoute was a semester project for a UX class at the University of North Texas, with Rosly Tavarez, Anaya Smith and Alyssa Cuarenta. We researched together, from interviews to the design studio. I turned that research into the wireframes, the brand and the working prototype, then ran the heuristic evaluation.

This is the work so far. We haven't tested it with pantry staff yet, and the case study says where that leaves gaps.

Step 1

Food arrives, and nobody saw it coming

Texas has the highest food insecurity rate in the country: 6.06 million people, about 1 in 5. Food pantries are the last step between donated food and the families who need it, and they run on very little staff time.

We started from food waste and narrowed to one handoff: food moving from a parent food bank and local grocery stores into a pantry. That's where time, food and trust get lost.

  1. Find the pain points in how a pantry receives food today.
  2. Design something that fits the workflow pantries already have. The ordering portal, the delivery windows and the receiving steps stay. FreshRoute makes them visible.
Step 2

We went to the pantry

We used five methods: a contextual inquiry and interview with the coordinator of the Diamond Eagle Basic Needs Hub at UNT, an interview with a manager and driver at Tarrant Area Food Bank, a competitive feature analysis of four apps, a heuristic audit of Careit (the donation platform pantries use, 40 findings), and desk research on how parent food banks supply their pantries.

The coordinator showed us the shelves, the six fridge and freezer units and the food bank's ordering portal. The Hub sees about 600 visits a week, and a truck from Tarrant Area Food Bank arrives every Thursday, "usually around 10 or 11, depends on their traffic."

  • Deliveries arrive blind

    "You could be sending an important email and then it's like, oh, the truck's here." The pantry waits out a whole delivery window with no live arrival time.

  • Cold food needs proof

    "Unless they're actively timing when they took it out of the fridge, we don't know how long it's been out, so we can't accept it."

  • Drivers can't see ahead either

    The food bank driver can't see a pickup before he gets there, and blankets are his only cold storage, which limits how far he can go.

Too Good To Go, HelloFresh, Plentiful, Misfits MarketDelivery tracking exists, but only for consumers waiting on a meal kit. Nothing tracks food going into a pantry.

Step 3

Design for the coordinator, design against shrinkage

Our persona, Maya, is built from the interview and the contextual inquiry. She runs a pantry with about 600 visits a week, one other staff member and rotating volunteers, and orders 3,000 to 6,000 lb a week. Her pain points became our design drivers: warn before the truck arrives, time cold food from the moment it's loaded, and show quantities and storage needs up front.

Our anti-persona, Max, is a trusted volunteer who quietly resells food that isn't tracked. We didn't design to catch people. We designed to close the gaps: counts at pickup must match drop-off, every receipt records who signed, and the drive record shows when things happened. The same record protects honest drivers.

  • Pantry coordinator

    The primary user. Decides whether a tool gets adopted.

  • Volunteer drivers

    Pick up from stores. They're recorded, so the app has to help them too.

  • Parent food bank

    Delivers the weekly order, and takes receipts and credit requests.

  • Grocery stores

    Only need an arrival time and a "ready at the dock" button, with no account.

Step 4

Sketch, vote, combine

From the journey map we wrote two need statements. The one we pursued: pantry managers need a way to monitor deliveries so they can predict arrival, prepare, and make sure donations arrive reliably.

Each of us sketched alone, then we dot-voted and combined the strongest ideas. The top votes were colour that creates urgency, driver input that feeds store descriptions, multiple destinations and useful metrics. Gamification was voted out, because it distracts and stresses drivers.

On the impact and feasibility matrix, live arrival notifications landed in the no-brainer corner. "Handy Footage", a camera that explains damage, was cheap to build but only explains problems after they happen, so it became a supporting drive record.

Step 5

Grayscale first, then the real thing

Before any visual design, I laid out both flows as annotated mid-fidelity wireframes, 26 pages in all: a driver from finding food to the drive report, and a manager seeing what's coming and checking what happened. They're grayscale on purpose, so feedback stayed on structure and content.

Research moved us during the build. The wireframes said "food bank manager", but the pantry coordinator turned out to be the real user, so the pantry's Incoming screen now leads with the food bank truck, and I added a receiving flow.

Step 6

A Thursday delivery, end to end

The prototype is a working iPad web app with three roles: the pantry, a volunteer driver and the food bank driver. The main scenario follows a real Thursday.

  1. The coordinator adds this week's order from the portal confirmation.
  2. The truck leaves the warehouse in Fort Worth, and the pantry watches it come in against its delivery window.
  3. About 15 minutes out, the iPad gives a heads-up: bring the pallet blanket for the cold lines.
  4. At the door, a volunteer checks each line. One case of apples arrived crushed.
  5. FreshRoute turns that into a credit request, due within 3 business days.

Volunteer pickup runs work the same way. The driver plans a route, the store gets a link with the arrival time and a "ready at the dock" button, and the run ends in a report that explains itself in a sentence.

Step 7

Keep the promise, show the proof

The brand is a calm, friendly dispatcher. The icon is a ring that's nearly closed, with a leaf: the arrival ring from the app. One green, Sprout, is the only brand colour, and it also means on time. Amber means slipping, coral means late, and cold food gets its own blue. Every status colour comes with a glyph and a word.

The first dark mode felt "like a computer terminal", so the green-black neutrals became soft charcoal and monospace left the interface. Gamification, streaks and confetti are ruled out, and so is decorative motion while the vehicle is moving.

Step 8

Evaluated, then fixed

We couldn't run sessions with pantry staff before the deadline, so we used our fallback: an expert review of the live prototype on an iPad against Nielsen's ten heuristics, across all three flows. It found no catastrophes, one major issue, six minor and four cosmetic.

After the review, I fixed the major issue and four of the minor ones in the live app.

FindingSeverityFix

Step 9

What we haven't verified

An expert review shows where a design breaks the rules. It can't show how a volunteer behaves at the door on a busy Thursday.

  1. Usability testing with pantry staff and volunteers. The facilitator guide is written and ready.
  2. A real delivery. The truck's location comes from a stand-in "I drive for the food bank" role. In practice it would likely come from the food bank's own fleet tracking.
  3. Importing orders straight from the food bank's portal, instead of typing the invoice lines.
  4. A short first-run walkthrough for each role. So far there's only a hint on a driver's first run.

The decision I'd repeat is going to the pantry. One afternoon there moved the whole design, from a dashboard for a food bank manager to an arrival time for the people waiting at the door.
