Taglet

A browser app that converts, tags and groups your own music into albums that hold together in Spotify Local Files, without a single file leaving your device.

Step 1

Music Spotify doesn't have

Spotify can play files from your own device, but only if they're tagged well enough for it to group them. People who have music Spotify doesn't, like demos, DJ edits, rips and their own bounces, end up with albums split into pieces, missing covers and artists shown as raw strings.

The design question became: how do you make tagging audio for Spotify feel like one calm task instead of three tools and a forum thread?

The product brief set the shape of the work:

  1. The core task is short: drop files, fix titles, artists and cover, see "Ready for Spotify", export.
  2. The promise comes first: files never leave the device. The one exception, Link a device, is end-to-end encrypted.
  3. It works on a phone and a laptop, because the audio is usually on one and the Spotify app is on the other.
  4. It's trustworthy before it's clever. Dark, a little playful and plain-spoken about what it does with your files.
Step 2

Everyone edits tags, nobody checks the album

I compared the tools people already use for this job. They're good at what they're built for, but none was built for getting a pile of unreleased audio into Spotify.

Tool typeStrengthGap for this job
Desktop tag editors (Mp3tag, Picard, Kid3)Every field, batch actions, lookupsDesktop only, no conversion, and no idea why a Spotify album splits
Mobile tag editor (Evertag)Clean, readable, batch modeiOS only, no conversion, no album-consistency check
Drag-and-drop bridges (WALTR PRO)Converts and fills metadata on the way inPaid, built for Apple's apps, needs a cable or bridge
Online convertersNothing to installYour unreleased music is uploaded to a stranger's server, with ads, size caps and tags as an afterthought

  • Check the whole album

    Every tool edits tags. None tells you the album will split, because a guest artist with no album artist, or one cover that differs, breaks the group.

  • One trip, not three

    People convert in one tool, then find the converter stripped the tags. Converting and tagging belong in one place.

  • Privacy as a fact

    Running everything in the browser means the claim can be checked in the network panel instead of taken on faith.

Step 3

The album is the unit, not the file

The central decision was where each field lives. Album fields (title, album artist, year, genre, compilation, cover) are stored once on the album and written identically to every track, so they can't drift apart. Track fields (title, artists, track and disc number) sit on the track.

Beside the album sits a live check. It reads "Ready for Spotify", or it lists what's wrong, each problem with a one-tap fix: fill in the album artist, number tracks in order, add a cover. The tag hanging off the cover is the same one in the logo, so the status feels like part of the object instead of a warning banner.

Step 4

A tag on a string in a dark room

A taglet is a small tag. The brand is a patina-green tag hanging on a string: warm, handled, a little playful and plain-spoken. Dark suits the use scene, sorting music late on a laptop or in bed on a phone, and it lets album art be the brightest thing on screen.

The accent is Patina, the blue-green copper turns with age. I picked it over a first-draft manila gold, and it stays clear of Spotify's green on purpose. A second colour, Signal, appears only when something needs fixing. The usual "AI app" accents (indigo-violet gradients, neon on black) were ruled out.

Glass is only on the floating control layer, and the current album's art is blurred into the room behind it, so the bars take on that album's colours. Content is never glass, and lists are lists, not card grids.

  • Mood

    Warm, calm and handled, with a little play.

  • Anchor

    A tag on a string. It leans toward the pointer, swings when files arrive and knocks once when an album is ready.

  • Audience

    People with music Spotify doesn't have, on a phone and a laptop.

  • Voice

    Plain words, sentence case, second person. Buttons say what will happen.

Step 5

Calls that shaped it

These are the decisions I'd defend in a review, and why I made them.

  • One field per artist

    Tools write "A feat. B", "A/B" or "A; B" inconsistently, and Spotify shows the raw string. Each artist gets their own field, written as "A, B, C" the way Spotify shows its own catalogue. A preview line shows the exact string, and one button applies the same artists to every track.

  • MP3, and say why

    "Which format does Spotify want?" is a forum question. Taglet converts to MP3 at 320 kbps (or V0) and explains why in one sentence. MP3s you add are only re-tagged, never re-encoded, so they keep their quality.

  • Gapless by default

    Encoders add silence at the start and end of a track. Sources are decoded with that removed and every MP3 carries a gapless header, so an album meant to flow doesn't click between songs. An optional switch trims silent edges.

  • Untagged files form one album

    The first audit showed that untagged files dropped together each became their own album, which worked against the main promise. They now form one album, and a lone untagged song becomes a single named after itself.

  • New builds replace, not duplicate

    Drop an updated render of a song you've already tagged and Taglet matches it by file name or title. You confirm before anything is replaced, and the new audio keeps the title, artists, number, album and cover, so Spotify replaces the old file. Every replacement can be undone.

  • Link a device instead of a cloud

    The audio is on the laptop, the cover is in the camera roll and the files need to reach the phone. A QR code or a 10-character code pairs two browsers and sends songs or images either way, end-to-end encrypted. The relay only sees ciphertext.

  • Behave like an app on a phone

    It installs to the home screen, works offline and, once installed, drops zoom and pull-to-refresh and uses bottom sheets you drag to dismiss. On a phone the album cover morphs between the list and the album.

Step 6

No framework, no build step

Taglet is static files and one small Cloudflare Worker. Decoding and encoding run in ffmpeg.wasm on the visitor's device, and a tag writer, a metadata parser and a store-only zip writer are plain JavaScript with their own tests. The Worker relays encrypted device links, stores encrypted share links for 30 days and handles optional sign-in.

Privacy is built into the architecture, not only the copy. Share links are encrypted on the device with AES-GCM before upload, and the key lives in the link's fragment, which a server never sees. Usage is counted anonymously with no cookies, and no file names, titles or emails are ever stored.

  • 30 daysShare links kept, as ciphertext only
  • 9+Input formats converted to MP3
Step 7

Audited, fixed, measured again

I audited the live site on 10 November 2026 at 390 by 844 (touch) and 1440 by 900, against WCAG 2.2 AA, Nielsen's ten heuristics and my own brand sheet, using automated probes plus keyboard and reading passes. The first pass found six issues. All six are fixed and re-measured.

Step 8

What I haven't verified

An audit finds inconsistency and broken rules. It can't say whether the album check actually calms someone down, and some things I haven't been able to test yet.

  1. A real iPhone and a real Android phone. Phone checks used Chrome's touch emulation at 390px.
  2. Link a device across two networks. I tested it between two tabs on one machine. It relays through Cloudflare, so network address translation shouldn't matter, but I haven't measured cellular to Wi-Fi.
  3. How Spotify itself displays the exported files. I checked the tags by reading them back with ffmpeg.
  4. Usability testing with people who actually have unreleased music to get into Spotify.

The decision I'd repeat is making the album the unit. Fields stored once, a check on the whole album and a status that's part of the cover made the rest of the interface simpler. The weak spot is that I audited my own work. If I did it again, I'd put it in front of real musicians before writing the audit.
